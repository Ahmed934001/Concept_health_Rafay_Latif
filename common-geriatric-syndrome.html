Understanding older-adult health

Common Geriatric Syndrome

A

  • Agitation

    Restlessness, irritability, or emotional distress that may show as pacing, repeated movements, or verbal outbursts.

  • Anorexia of aging

    Reduced appetite and food intake in later life, which can contribute to weight loss and poor nutrition.

  • Aspiration

    Food, liquid, saliva, or stomach contents entering the airway instead of passing safely into the stomach.

B

  • Balance impairment

    Difficulty staying steady while sitting, standing, or moving, which can increase the risk of falling.

  • Bowel incontinence

    Unintentional leakage of stool or difficulty controlling bowel movements.

  • Bone loss

    A reduction in bone tissue or density that can weaken bones and increase fracture risk.

C

  • Cognitive impairment

    Difficulty with memory, attention, language, reasoning, or other thinking skills, ranging from mild to severe.

  • Constipation

    Bowel movements that are infrequent, hard, difficult to pass, or leave a feeling of incomplete emptying.

  • Chronic pain

    Pain that persists or keeps returning for more than three months and may affect sleep, movement, and daily life.

D

  • Delirium

    A sudden, fluctuating change in attention, awareness, and thinking, often triggered by illness or medication. It needs urgent medical assessment.

  • Dementia

    A decline in memory and other thinking abilities severe enough to interfere with everyday activities and independence.

  • Depression

    Persistent low mood or loss of interest that can affect sleep, appetite, energy, concentration, and everyday functioning.

E

  • Elder abuse/neglect

    Harm, exploitation, or failure to meet an older adult's basic care needs, often within a relationship of trust.

  • Excessive daytime sleepiness

    An unusual tendency to doze off or struggle to stay awake during normal daytime activities.

  • Exhaustion (frailty)

    Persistent low energy or unusual tiredness that may be one feature of frailty and limit everyday activity.

F

  • Falls

    Unintentionally coming to rest on the ground, floor, or another lower surface.

  • Frailty

    Reduced physical reserves that make a person more vulnerable to illness, injury, and loss of independence.

  • Functional decline

    A reduced ability to manage everyday tasks, such as bathing, dressing, preparing meals, or taking medicines.

G

  • Gait disorders

    Changes in walking pattern, speed, or coordination that make walking difficult or unsafe.

  • Grip weakness

    Reduced hand strength that makes it harder to hold objects, open containers, or perform daily tasks.

  • Grief/bereavement

    Grief is the emotional response to loss; bereavement is the period following the death of someone close.

H

  • Hearing impairment

    Reduced ability to hear sounds or understand speech, which can make communication and daily activities harder.

  • Hospital-associated disability

    New or worsened difficulty with daily activities that develops during or following a hospital stay.

  • Hyponatremia

    A lower-than-normal sodium level in the blood, which can affect brain and muscle function.

I

  • Incontinence

    Unintentional loss of urine or stool because bladder or bowel control is reduced.

  • Immobility

    A major limitation in moving independently, changing position, or walking.

  • Insomnia

    Difficulty falling asleep, staying asleep, or returning to sleep despite having enough opportunity to sleep.

J

  • Joint pain (osteoarthritis)

    Pain and stiffness associated with osteoarthritis, a condition involving changes in cartilage and other joint tissues.

  • Joint contractures

    Persistent tightening of muscles, tendons, or other tissues that limits how far a joint can move.

  • Judgment impairment

    Difficulty weighing choices, understanding consequences, or making safe and appropriate decisions.

K

  • Kyphosis

    An excessive forward curve of the upper spine that creates a rounded or stooped back.

  • Kidney function decline

    Reduced ability of the kidneys to filter waste and maintain the body's fluid and mineral balance.

  • Knee-related mobility loss

    Difficulty walking, climbing stairs, or rising from a chair because of knee pain, stiffness, weakness, or instability.

L

  • Loneliness

    The distressing feeling that one's social connections are fewer or less meaningful than desired.

  • Lightheadedness

    A feeling of faintness or near-fainting, rather than a sensation that the surroundings are spinning.

  • Lower urinary tract symptoms

    Problems with storing or passing urine, such as urgency, frequent urination, a weak stream, or incomplete emptying.

M

  • Malnutrition

    An inadequate, excessive, or unbalanced supply of nutrients; in older adults, insufficient energy and protein are common concerns.

  • Memory loss

    Difficulty remembering information or experiences, which can have many causes and does not always mean dementia.

  • Mobility impairment

    Difficulty moving around safely and independently, including walking, changing position, or transferring between a bed and chair.

N

  • Nocturia

    Waking from sleep during the night to pass urine.

  • Nutritional deficiency

    Too little of one or more essential nutrients because of inadequate intake, poor absorption, or increased needs.

  • Nighttime confusion (sundowning)

    Confusion, restlessness, or agitation that begins or worsens in the late afternoon or evening, often in people with dementia.

O

  • Orthostatic hypotension

    A drop in blood pressure after standing up that may cause dizziness, lightheadedness, or fainting.

  • Osteoporosis

    A condition in which reduced bone strength makes fractures more likely, sometimes after only a minor injury.

  • Oral/dental problems

    Conditions affecting the teeth, gums, mouth, or dentures that may interfere with eating, speaking, or comfort.

P

  • Polypharmacy

    Use of multiple medicines, often defined as five or more, which can increase the risk of interactions and side effects.

  • Pressure injuries

    Damage to skin and underlying tissue from sustained pressure, sometimes combined with friction-related shearing, often over bony areas.

  • Postural instability

    Difficulty maintaining or recovering a steady body position while standing or moving.

Q

  • Quality-of-life decline

    A worsening in a person's sense of well-being, comfort, independence, or ability to enjoy meaningful activities.

  • Quadriceps weakness

    Reduced strength in the muscles at the front of the thigh, making standing, walking, or climbing stairs harder.

  • Quiet (hypoactive) delirium

    Delirium with reduced activity, sleepiness, or withdrawal rather than agitation. This sudden change also needs urgent medical assessment.

R

  • Recurrent falls

    Repeated episodes of falling, which may signal ongoing problems with balance, strength, medicines, or health.

  • Restless legs syndrome

    An urge to move the legs, often with unpleasant sensations, that worsens at rest and at night and improves with movement.

  • Urinary retention

    Difficulty emptying the bladder completely, or an inability to pass urine despite a full bladder.

S

  • Sarcopenia

    Loss of muscle strength and muscle mass that can reduce physical ability and increase the risk of falls.

  • Sleep disturbance

    Disruption in sleep timing, duration, or quality that can affect daytime alertness and well-being.

  • Syncope

    A brief loss of consciousness caused by a temporary reduction in blood flow to the brain, commonly called fainting.

T

  • Tremor

    Involuntary, rhythmic shaking of a body part, often affecting the hands, head, or voice.

  • Tinnitus

    Hearing ringing, buzzing, or other sounds when there is no corresponding external sound.

  • Thermoregulation problems

    Difficulty keeping body temperature within a safe range, increasing vulnerability to excessive heat or cold.

U

  • Urinary incontinence

    Unintentional leakage of urine, ranging from occasional drops to a larger loss of bladder control.

  • Undernutrition

    Insufficient energy, protein, or other nutrients to meet the body's needs and maintain health.

  • Unsteady gait

    Walking that feels unstable or poorly balanced, increasing the chance of stumbling or falling.

V

  • Vision impairment

    Reduced ability to see clearly, distinguish detail, or use the full field of vision.

  • Vertigo

    A false sensation that you or your surroundings are spinning or moving.

  • Vitamin D/B12 deficiency

    Low vitamin D can affect bones and muscles; low vitamin B12 can affect blood cells, nerves, and thinking.

W

  • Weight loss (unintentional)

    Loss of body weight without trying, which may reflect reduced intake, illness, or other health changes.

  • Wandering

    Moving about or leaving a familiar place in a way that may lead to becoming lost, sometimes associated with dementia.

  • Weakness

    Reduced muscle strength that makes usual movements or everyday tasks harder to perform.

X

  • Xerostomia (dry mouth)

    A persistent feeling of mouth dryness, often related to reduced saliva, medicines, or an underlying condition.

  • Xerosis (dry skin)

    Skin that lacks moisture and may feel rough, itchy, scaly, or cracked.

  • Xerophthalmia (dry eye)

    Abnormal dryness of the eye surface, classically associated with vitamin A deficiency; ordinary dry eye can have other causes.

Y

  • Yeast infections (oral thrush)

    Overgrowth of Candida yeast in the mouth that may cause white patches, redness, soreness, or swallowing discomfort.

  • Yellow skin (jaundice, drug- or liver-related)

    Yellowing of the skin or whites of the eyes from excess bilirubin, potentially linked to liver, bile duct, blood, or medication problems.

  • Yo-yo weight fluctuation

    Repeated cycles of weight loss and regain; this describes a weight pattern rather than a specific diagnosis.

Z

  • Zinc deficiency

    Too little zinc in the body, which can affect taste, appetite, immune function, and wound healing.

  • Zoster/postherpetic neuralgia

    Zoster is shingles, a painful rash caused by reactivated chickenpox virus; postherpetic neuralgia is nerve pain that persists afterward.
